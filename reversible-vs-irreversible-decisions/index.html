One-Way and Two-Way Door Decisions

By Chase Arenella · Updated November 16, 2026 · 2 min read

In one line

A one-way door decision is hard or costly to undo. A two-way door decision can be reversed cheaply. Sorting choices this way tells you how much process each one deserves.

▶ Watch the 70-second video version

The two doors

A two-way door is a choice you can walk back through. If it goes badly, you reverse it, and the cost is small. A one-way door is a choice that is hard, expensive, or impossible to undo. Once you are through, you live with the result.

Most decisions are two-way doors. Teams often treat them like one-way doors anyway, so they add reviews, meetings, and sign-offs to choices that a quick test would settle. The goal is not to decide faster everywhere. It is to spend process where it buys something.

How to sort a choice

Reversibility is a matter of degree, so ask a few direct questions before you pick a process. Answer them in order and stop when you have a clear read.

  1. What does it cost to undo this, in money, time, and trust?
  2. How long until we would know it was wrong?
  3. Who else is affected, and can they absorb a reversal?
  4. Can we shrink it into a smaller, reversible version first?

Matching process to the door

For two-way doors, name one owner, set a short deadline, and decide with the information at hand. Write down what would make you reverse it. Then act. A wrong call you catch in a week costs less than a right call you reach in a month.

For one-way doors, slow down on purpose. Gather outside views, ask someone to argue the opposite case, and record the reasoning in a decision log. AI can help by surfacing options and risks, but the final call stays with a named person. Competitive players make the same split: adjusting positioning mid-round is cheap, while committing every resource to a single round is not.

Common mistakes

The sorting step can go wrong in predictable ways. Watch for these patterns.

  • False one-way doors. Fear makes a reversible choice feel permanent. Ask what reversing it would actually take.
  • Hidden one-way doors. A small pilot becomes permanent once customers, contracts, or public promises depend on it.
  • Slow reversals. A door is only two-way if you notice the problem in time. Long feedback delays quietly lock it.
  • Never closing the door. Reversible does not mean endlessly revisited. Set a review date and hold the decision until then.

Common questions

What is a one-way door decision?

It is a choice that is costly or impossible to reverse, such as a major hire, a public commitment, or a platform migration. These deserve slower, more careful review.

How do you know if a decision is reversible?

Estimate the cost and time to undo it, and how soon you would see it failing. If both are low, treat it as a two-way door.
